## Verbatim quotes
Block quotes are Dow's text as printed by Bishop. Ellipses inside Bishop's quotes were dropped by splitting into separate quotes. Figures such as 1/2 and 3/8 are as printed.

- Q1: "The stock market has three movements. It has a daily fluctuation ordinarily running over from 1/2 to 1 1/2 points. It has a longer swing frequently through a period of about 20 to 40 days. It has its main movement which extends over a period of years." — 12 May 1899 (Bishop p.105)
- Q2: "Any close student of market swings discovers that the stock market has three movements which are separate yet united." — 15 Jan 1900 (p.105)
- Q3: "A move of this character usually requires from 20 to 40 days for its consummation." — 15 Jan 1900 (p.105; text as printed "usually re-quires from 20 to 40 days")
- Q4: "The market is always to be considered as having three movements, all going on at the same time. The first is the narrow movement from day to day. The second is the short swing, running from two weeks to a month or more; the third is the main movement covering at least four years in its duration." — "December 20, 1900" per Bishop (Hamilton and Nelson give 19 Dec) (p.106)
- Q5: "Nothing is more certain than that the market has three well-defined movements which fit into each other." — 4 Jan 1902 (p.106)
- Q6: "The secondary movement covers a period ranging from ten days to sixty days, averaging probably between thirty and forty days. The third movement is the great swing covering from four to six years." — 4 Jan 1902 (p.106)
- Q7: "It is a law of the market swings that large primary movements shall have large reverses. The bigger the advance, the bigger the reaction sooner or later. The ordinary development is that the secondary movement is from three-eighths to five-eighths of the amount of the primary movement." — 16 May 1899 (p.117)
- Q8: "It is a remarkable fact in speculation that both the average price of a number of stocks and the price of individual stocks show strong tendencies, both in rallies and relapses to reach one-half of the primary movement." — 7 Jul 1900 (p.118)
- Q9: "One of the best general rules in speculation is the theory that reaction in an advance or a decline will be at least one-half of the primary movement." — 19 Oct 1900 (p.119)
- Q10: "The ordinary rule of reaction in a rising market is that it will be from three-eighths to five-eighths of the amount of the advance." — 24 Nov 1900 (p.119)
- Q11: "The more general course is a sagging movement in which prices work off for from 15 to 30 days." — 24 Nov 1900 (p.119)
- Q12: "It often happens that the secondary movement in a market amounts to 3/8 to 1/2 of the primary move." — 22 Jan 1901 (p.120, quoted by Bishop)
- Q13: "Whoever will study our averages, as given daily in The Journal for years past, will see how uniformly periods of advance have been followed by periods of decline, amounting in a large proportion of cases, to from one-third to one-half of the rise." — 30 Jan 1901 (p.120)
- Q14: "It is a theory of speculation that even in a bull market the relapses will be approximately half the amount of the advances." — 27 Mar 1901 (p.120)
- Q15: "A person watching the tide coming in and who wishes to know the spot which marks the high tide, sets a stick in the sand at the points reached by the incoming waves until the stick reaches a position where the waves do not come up to it, and finally recede enough to show that the tide has turned." — "Watching the Tide", 31 Jan 1901 (pp.111-112)
- Q16: "This method holds good in watching and determining the flood tide of the stock market. The average of twenty stocks is the peg which marks the height of the waves." — 31 Jan 1901 (p.112)
- Q17: "It is certain that the high point in September was not quite as high as the high point in April 1899. The high point in April 1900 was not quite as high as the highest in the September or April preceding. This is the sign of a bear market." — 9 May 1900 (p.111)
- Q18: "It is noticeable that industrials and rails have risen in the present period almost exactly the same amount." — 22 Apr 1899 (p.113)
- Q19: "For the period under review there is no mention by Dow of confirmation, but several editorials bear upon the movement of both averages in a general fashion." — Bishop's comment, not Dow (p.113)
- Q20: "He does not expand upon this observation, and it seems logical to assume that if confirmation of the averages was a tenet of Dow's theory he would have made further comment along these lines." — Bishop on Dow's 14 May 1902 editorial (p.117)
- Q21: "Dow recognized that the market at times moved in a narrow range, but he never defined a "line" formation as such in the Dow-Jones averages." — Bishop's comment (p.140)
- Q22: "The range has been within 1 per cent, showing very clearly the narrowness of trading." — 19 Feb 1900, on the 20-stock average (p.141)
- Q23: "The average has swung between 81.50 and 82.40 since the 26th of March." — 5 Apr 1900 (p.141)
- Q24: "The narrowness of the market is entirely normal." — 17 Jan 1900 (p.141)
- Q25: "In a bull market, dullness is generally followed by advances; in a bear market by decline." — 7 Mar 1902 (p.143)
- Q26: "The longer time, moreover, that the market remains dull the more important is the movement likely to be." — 13 Oct 1899 (p.141)
- Q27: "A market moves by its momentum. The momentum represented by transactions from 700,000 shares to 1,500,000 shares a day, is obviously greater than the momentum represented by transactions only half as large." — 19 Mar 1901 (p.139)
- Q28: "There is a relation between the volume of business and the movement of prices." — 29 May 1901 (p.140)
- Q29: "If tomorrow or next day, the market is found heavy in tone, but with a small volume of business, it will suggest that manipulators are putting prices down without getting stocks." — 21 Jan 1902 (p.140)
- Q30: "The stock market discounts tendencies. Stocks went up before the improvement in business became pronounced." — 10 May 1900 (p.107)
- Q31: "There is one qualifying point not to be overlooked. This is that the stock market discounts expected changes in condition." — 28 Feb 1902 (p.108)
- Q32: "The stock market is in the nature of a barometer which reflects the rise and fall in general conditions." — 13 May 1899 (p.93)
- Q33: "No one can ever say that the top of the market has been reached except by looking back at a high point." — 11 Feb 1902 (p.138)
- Q34: "The first thing that is necessary to note is that in dealing with the stock market there is no way of telling when the top of an advance or the bottom of a decline has been reached until some time after such top or bottom has been made." — 14 May 1902 (pp.138-139)
- Q35: "The one thing sure in speculation is that values determine prices in the long run. Manipulation is effective temporarily, but the investor establishes prices in the end." — 25 Feb 1902 (p.122)
- Q36: "The reactions which come from day to day are not significant." — 29 Jun 1899 (p.125)
- Q37: "Mr. Dow never mentioned volume in his editorials on stock movements." — Leffler, quoted by Bishop and then rebutted (p.139)

## Notes
Second-hand: Bishop selected and sometimes paraphrased; ellipses inside the Bishop quotes were removed by splitting. Dates are Bishop's and differ from other sources for the three-movements editorial (20 Dec vs 19 Dec 1900). Bishop 1960 is in copyright and was read from a third-party host. Bishop also states Dow wrote no "Dow Theory" himself (Russell: Dow never used the term). Dow's retracement in 1899 to 1901 is three-eighths to five-eighths or one-half, which differs from Rhea's 33 to 66 percent. A fuller primary compilation (Dow Theory Unplugged, Sether) was not accessible.
