## Verbatim quotes
- Q1: "He showed that there are, simultaneously, three movements in progress in the stock market." — Ch. I, p.4
- Q2: "It will be shown that this primary movement tends to run over a period of at least a year and is generally much longer." — Ch. I, p.5
- Q3: "Coincident with it, or in the course of it, is Dow's secondary movement, represented by sharp rallies in a primary bear market and sharp reactions in a primary bull market." — Ch. I, p.5
- Q4: "Concurrently with the primary and secondary movement of the market, and constant throughout, there obviously was, as Dow pointed out, the underlying fluctuation from day to day." — Ch. I, p.6
- Q5: "There have been unnecessary attempts to combine the volume of sales and to read the average with reference to commodity index numbers. But it must be obvious that the averages have already taken those things into account, just as the barometer considers everything which affects the weather." — Ch. I, pp.7-8 (sentence crosses a page break)
- Q6: "Nothing is more certain than that the market has three well defined movements which fit into each other. The first is the daily variation due to local causes and the balance of buying or selling at that particular time. The secondary movement covers a period ranging from ten days to sixty days, averaging probably between thirty and forty days. The third swing is the great move covering from four to six years." — Ch. III, p.23, Hamilton quoting Dow, The Wall Street Journal, Jan 4, 1902, "Swings Within Swings"
- Q7: "The extent of the primary movement, as given in this quotation, is proved to be far too long by subsequent experience; and a careful examination has shown me that the major swing before Dow wrote was never "from four to six years," rarely three years and oftener less than two." — Ch. III, pp.23-24
- Q8: "The market is always to be considered as having three movements, all going on at the same time. The first is the narrow movement from day to day. The second is the short swing, running from two weeks to a month or more; the third is the main movement, covering at least four years in its duration." — Ch. IV, p.30, Hamilton quoting Dow's editorial of Dec 19, 1900
- Q9: "It has already been shown that his third and main movement may complete itself in much less than Dow's assumed four years" — Ch. IV, p.30
- Q10: "There come times when a stock with a good degree of activity will stay within a narrow range of prices, say two points, until there has formed quite a long horizontal line of these figures. The formation of such a line sometimes suggests that stock has been accumulated or distributed, and this leads other people to buy or sell at the same time." — Ch. IV, p.35, Hamilton quoting Dow's editorial of Jul 20, 1901 (the "book method")
- Q11: "A much more practicable theory is that founded on the law of action and reaction. It seems to be a fact that a primary movement in the market will generally have a secondary movement in the opposite direction of at least three-eighths of the primary movement. If a stock advances ten points, it is very likely to have a relapse of four points or more." — Ch. IV, p.36, Hamilton quoting Dow, Jul 20, 1901
- Q12: "It is impossible to tell in advance the length of any primary movement, but the further it goes, the greater the reaction when it comes, hence the more certainty of being able to trade successfully on that reaction." — Ch. IV, p.36, Hamilton quoting Dow, Jul 20, 1901
- Q13: "The average duration of seven major bull swings is twenty-five months; while the average duration of seven major bear swings is fifteen months." — Ch. V, p.45 (period 1900-1923, from the list of fifteen swings)
- Q14: "The stock market barometer is not perfect, or, to put it more correctly, the adolescent science of reading it is far from having attained perfection." — Ch. VI, p.54
- Q15: "We can satisfy ourselves from examples that a period of trading within a narrow range what we have called a "line" gaining significance as the number of trading days increases, can only mean accumulation or distribution, and that the subsequent price movement shows whether the market has become bare of stocks or saturated with an oversupply." — Ch. VII, p.60 (OCR drops the dashes around "what we have called a 'line'")
- Q16: "News known is news discounted. What everybody knows has ceased to be a market factor, except in the rare instance of a panic, when the stock market is confessedly taken by surprise." — Ch. VII, p.61
- Q17: "But remembering that the averages must confirm each other, The Wall Street Journal said, on August 25th:" — Ch. VII, p.62
- Q18: "In a primary bull or bear market the actuating forces are above and beyond manipulation. But in the other movements of Dow's theory, a secondary reaction in a bull market or the corresponding secondary rally in a bear market, or in the third movement (the daily fluctuation) which goes on all the time, there is room for manipulation, but only in individual stocks, or in small groups, with a well-recognized leading issue." — Ch. VIII, p.72
- Q19: "It is worth while to note here that the volume of trading is always larger in a bull market than in a bear market. It expands as prices go up and contracts as they decline." — Ch. XII, p.136, "Relevance of the Volume of Trading"
- Q20: "This is uniformly true of major bull swings, but is subject to great modification in the secondary movements. A sharp reaction in a bull market will often stimulate the volume of business." — Ch. XII, pp.136-137
- Q21: "One of the shortest ways of going wrong is to accept an indication by one average which has not been clearly confirmed by the other." — Ch. XII, p.138
- Q22: "Dow estimated the length of a counter movement at from forty to sixty days, but subsequent experience has shown that this longer range is exceedingly rare and that the duration may be appreciably less than forty days." — Ch. XIII, p.143
- Q23: "It is a tried rule, which will help to guide us in studying the secondary movement, that the change in the broad general direction of the market is abrupt, while the resumption of the major movement is appreciably slower. The latter is frequently foretold by a line of accumulation in a bull market or a line of distribution in a bear market." — Ch. XIII, p.143
- Q24: "We have seen that a "line" in the average a succession of closing prices, over a sufficient number of days for a fair volume of trading within a narrow range must indicate either accumulation or distribution; and that a movement of the average price out of that line, downward or upward, will confidently indicate a change in the general market direction of at least a secondary and even a primary character, which we can depend upon where either average is confirmed by the other." — Ch. XIV, p.159 (OCR drops the dashes around the parenthetical)
- Q25: "We have also satisfied ourselves that the averages must confirm each other, although they may not break out of their respective lines on the same day or in the same week. It is sufficient if they take the same direction. It is by no means necessary, as experience shows, that the low or the high point of a primary movement should be made in both averages on the same day." — Ch. XIV, p.159

Additional rule-bearing passages not quoted (paraphrased, with location): Ch. XIII p.149 says a line in both averages followed by a price below the line would predict a secondary reaction, but experience shows the line usually comes after, not before, the secondary break; a new top above the origin of the secondary decline is "conclusive evidence" the bull movement has resumed. Ch. XIV p.160 says a double top "is by no means infallible, but is often useful". Ch. XV p.172-173: it is "axiomatic" that a line must be either accumulation or distribution; the 1914 line lasted 66 trading days in the industrials and 71 in the railroads, with extreme ranges of three and four points. Ch. XXI p.250-251: a line is "many days' trading and all within a range of three points or so". Ch. VIII (Q18) is the source for Rhea's later "manipulation" hypothesis. The chapter "A Turn in the Tide" is NOT in this book; it is Hamilton's WSJ editorial of Oct 25, 1929 (see rhea-1932-the-dow-theory and schannep-dow-theory-21st-century notes).

## Notes
- Primary source for the Dow-Hamilton line of the theory; the scanned text is the 1926 printing, so pages 250 onward (Ch. XXI) post-date 1922.
- Dow's words appear here only second-hand, as block quotes with WSJ dates (Dec 19, 1900; Jul 20, 1901; Jan 4, 1902; Jul 9, 1902). Those same editorials are reprinted in Nelson 1903 (see nelson-1903-abc-of-stock-speculation), where the wording matches except for numerals spelled out here ("ten points") versus digits there ("10 points").
- Hamilton gives no retracement fraction of his own; the "three-eighths" is Dow's and is stated for individual stocks, not the averages. The later "one-third to two-thirds" is Rhea's.
- No three-phase bull/bear schema and no volume-confirmation rule in the modern textbook form; Hamilton explicitly argues against adding volume to the averages (Q5), while elsewhere noting volume behaviour (Q19-Q20).
- OCR quality is good; the OCR drops em-dashes, which affects Q15 and Q24 punctuation. Page headers were stripped from quotes.
- Gutenberg #75687, suggested as an alternative, is Thomas Gibson's "The Cycles of Speculation", not Hamilton, so it was not used.
