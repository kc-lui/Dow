## Verbatim quotes
Marked (via Wiley/Schannep) or (via Blay). Schannep states the theorems are Rhea's text "altered only to the extent that they are somewhat better organized".

- Q1: "The fluctuations of the daily closing prices of the Dow-Jones Rail and Industrial averages afford a composite index of all the hopes, disappointments, and knowledge of everyone who knows anything of financial matters." — Hypothesis 2 (via Wiley/Schannep p.5)
- Q2: "The Dow Theory is not an infallible system for beating the market." — Hypothesis 3 (via Wiley/Schannep p.6)
- Q3: "There are three movements of the averages, all of which may be in progress at one and the same time." — Theorem, Dow's Three Movements (via Wiley/Schannep p.6)
- Q4: "The primary movement is the broad basic trend generally known as a bull or bear market extending over periods that have varied from less than a year to several years." — Primary movements (via Wiley/Schannep p.6)
- Q5: "a secondary reaction is considered to be an important decline in a bull market or advance in a bear market, usually lasting from three weeks to as many months, during which intervals the price movement generally retraces from 33 percent to 66 percent of the primary price change since the termination of the last preceding secondary reaction." — Secondary reaction (via Wiley/Schannep p.7; Blay cites Fraser p.52)
- Q6: "The movement of one price average must be confirmed by the other before reliable inferences may be drawn." — Both Averages Must Confirm (via Wiley/Schannep p.9)
- Q7: "a rally or a decline is one or more daily movements resulting in a net reversal of direction exceeding 3 percent of the price of either average." — Determining the Trend (via Wiley/Schannep p.9)
- Q8: "A "line" is a price movement extending two to three weeks or longer, during which period the price variation of both averages move within a range of approximately 5 percent." — Lines (via Wiley/Schannep p.10; Blay cites Fraser p.79)

Paraphrased from the same sources (not quoted, to stay within the cap):
- Hypothesis 1: manipulation is possible day to day, limited in secondaries, impossible for the primary trend (Wiley p.5).
- Primary movement: no known method of forecasting its extent or duration; its direction is the most important factor in speculation (Wiley p.6).
- Primary bear market: long downward movement interrupted by important rallies; three phases: abandonment of hopes, selling on decreased business and earnings, distress selling of sound securities (Wiley p.7).
- Primary bull market: broad upward movement interrupted by secondary reactions, averaging longer than two years; three phases: reviving confidence, response to improved earnings, rampant speculation (Wiley p.7).
- Secondaries are frequently mistaken for a change of primary trend (Wiley pp.7-8).
- Blay (via Fraser p.76) reports a second Rhea formulation that a normal secondary lasts three to twelve weeks and retraces one-third to two-thirds; and (Fraser p.61) that in his statistical work Rhea discarded reactions shorter than 15 days or smaller than 5 per cent of the averages.
- Daily fluctuations: inferences from one day are misleading and of little value except when lines are forming; still record them (Wiley p.8).
- Determining the trend: rallies exceeding prior highs with declines holding above prior lows are bullish; the reverse is bearish; movements need confirmation by both averages but not on the same day (Wiley p.9).
- Lines: indicate accumulation or distribution; simultaneous advance above the line predicts higher prices, simultaneous decline below predicts lower; one average alone is generally incorrect (Wiley p.10).
- Volume: an overbought market becomes dull on rallies and active on declines, and conversely; bull markets end in excessive activity and begin with light transactions (Wiley p.10).
- Double tops and bottoms are of little forecasting value (Wiley p.10).

## Notes
- Everything here is second-hand. The Wiley excerpt is a publisher-authorised reprint of Rhea's hypotheses and theorems inside Schannep's Chapter 1, so the wording is the most reliable available without the book itself; Schannep says he re-organised the theorems "somewhat".
- Rhea's text as printed by Schannep already contains the phrase "usually lasting from three weeks to as many months" with the "usually" that Blay stresses. Schannep's author's note lists Dow's and Hamilton's earlier duration ranges (two weeks to a month or more, Dec 19, 1900; ten to sixty days, Jan 4, 1902; 20 to 60 days, Sep 17, 1904; one to three months, Feb 26, 1909; a few days to many weeks, Feb 11, 1922), which shows the "three weeks" minimum is Rhea's, not Dow's or Hamilton's.
- The 3 % rally/decline threshold and the 5 % line width are the first quantitative filters in the Dow Theory lineage; neither is in Dow or Hamilton (compare hamilton-1922 and nelson-1903 notes).
- Blay's Fraser page numbers (52, 61, 76, 79) were not verified against any edition.
- Russell's "History of the Dow Theory" (see poitras-dow-theory-history note) paraphrases Rhea as "one-third to two-thirds" and "three weeks to three months", which is consistent with Q5 but not a quotation.
- Hamilton's "A Turn in the Tide" (Oct 25, 1929) is reprinted in the Wiley excerpt and is where "the barometer never indicates duration" appears.
